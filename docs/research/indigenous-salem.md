# The Indigenous history of Salem (Naumkeag) — research dossier

Compiled 2026-07-29, following the pattern of `indigenous-newbury.md`. Same accuracy bar
as the map: **nothing invented.** Where sources disagree, both readings are recorded here
and the in-game card says so.

> ⚠️ **Review status: NOT yet reviewed by an Indigenous reader.** Everything here comes
> from published secondary sources. Before this ships into a classroom it should be read
> by a Massachusett/Pawtucket reviewer, and ideally by Salem State University (which
> maintains a land acknowledgement) and the Salem Historical Society, which publishes an
> Indigenous community resource list. See "Open questions".

## 1. The name

**Naumkeag** was the Indigenous settlement where Salem now stands. The name is usually
translated **"fishing place"** — from *namaas* (fish) + *ki* (place) + *age* (at) — with
a second published reading of **"eel-land."** Both are in circulation; the card gives
"fishing place" and does not claim it is the only reading.

The English kept the name at first: the settlement was called Naumkeag from 1626 until it
was renamed Salem, from the Hebrew *shalom*, peace.

## 2. Who lived here — and a naming caution

The people of Naumkeag were **Pawtucket**, part of a confederation led by the sachem
**Nanepashemet** and, after him, his wife, whom the English recorded only as the
**Squaw Sachem of Mistick**. At the time of the Puritan migration (1620–1640) that
confederation held territory from the Charles River to the Merrimack.

⚠️ **The same caution as Newburyport applies.** Colonial documents refer to these people
variously as Naumkeag, Pawtucket, Penticut, Mystic or Wamesit, or simply by the name of
their current sachem. There is genuine unsettledness about the correct contemporary
endonym, and Salem State's land acknowledgement and the Salem Historical Society's
resource list are the right places to check current preferred usage before a classroom
version ships. **Do not present any one label as settled.**

Note also that "Squaw Sachem" is a **colonial record of her title, not her name** — the
English did not write down what she called herself. The word is now widely considered a
slur. The card names her as the English recorded her, says explicitly that her own name
was not written down, and does not use the word as a label for anyone else.

## 3. The people, in order

| person | English name | notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Nanepashemet** | — | sachem of the confederation; died **1619** |
| **the Squaw Sachem of Mistick** | — | his widow; governed the territories after his death, with their sons |
| **Wonohaquaham** | Sagamore John | Mystic/Winnisimmet |
| **Montowampate** | Sagamore James | Saugus |
| **Wenepoykin** | Sagamore George | **born 1616 (some sources 1620), sachem of Naumkeag, died 1684** |

**Wenepoykin** is the thread that carries Salem's Indigenous story all the way through the
colonial period, and it is the one most worth telling:

- Installed as sachem of Naumkeag while still a child.
- Survived the **1633 smallpox epidemic** that killed both his brothers and devastated the
  Pawtucket. (An earlier epidemic in **1618** had already struck Nanepashemet's people —
  the sequence matters: the land the English found "empty" in 1626 was emptied by disease
  within living memory.)
- Fought the English in **King Philip's War**; captured in **1676**.
- **Enslaved and shipped to Barbados**, where he spent **eight years**.
- Returned to Massachusetts in **1684** through the efforts of John Eliot, and died that
  same year at Rumney Marsh.

Sources describe him as the **last sachem of the Pawtucket**.

## 4. What the cards do with this

Six markers, placed first in `HISTORY` so the list does not open in 1626:

1. **`naumkeag`** — the name and what it means. Placed at the harbor.
2. **`squaw-sachem`** — Nanepashemet, his widow governing after 1619, and the plain fact
   that the English did not record her own name.
3. **`great-dying`** — the 1618 and 1633 epidemics. The land was not empty; it was emptied.
   ⚠️ Written carefully: this is the single most important corrective in the set, and it is
   also the heaviest. Kept factual, no imagery a third grader will carry home badly.
4. **`wenepoykin`** — the boy sachem who outlived his brothers, was sold to Barbados, and
   came home. Ends on his return, not on the enslavement.
5. **`fishing-place`** — the seasonal round and why a river mouth was worth a name.
6. **`still-here`** — present tense. Massachusett and Pawtucket descendants are here now;
   Salem State maintains a land acknowledgement.

**Voice rule carried over from Newburyport:** present tense where the people are present
tense. Nothing in the past tense that is not actually past.

## 5. Open questions — resolve before classroom use

1. **Preferred endonym.** Naumkeag? Pawtucket? Massachusett? Sources conflict and the
   communities' own current usage is what should govern. **Blocking for the pilot.**
2. **"Fishing place" vs "eel-land."** Both are published. Card uses the first and does not
   exclude the second — but a speaker should settle it.
3. **Wenepoykin's birth year** — 1616 and 1620 both appear. Card avoids stating one.
4. **How much of the Barbados enslavement to carry** at grade 3. Current draft states it
   plainly in one sentence and spends the rest of the card on his return. A reviewer may
   want it lighter or heavier; the fact is not negotiable, the framing is.
5. **The 1633 epidemic's scale** — sources say "decimated"; specific mortality figures for
   Naumkeag itself were not found. Card gives no number.
