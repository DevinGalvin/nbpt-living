# The Indigenous history of Newbury & Newburyport — research dossier

Compiled 2026-07-28 for the Newburyport 3rd-grade pilot. Same accuracy bar as the map:
**nothing invented.** Where sources disagree, both readings are recorded below and the
in-game card says so.

> ⚠️ **Review status: NOT yet reviewed by an Indigenous reader.** Everything here comes
> from published secondary sources. Before this ships into a classroom it should be read
> by the Museum of Old Newbury, the Newbury Historical Commission, and — ideally — an
> Abenaki/Pennacook reviewer. See "Open questions" at the bottom.

---

## 1. Who lived here

Algonquian-speaking people whom colonists grouped under the name **Pawtucket**, part of
the wider **Pennacook** world of the Merrimack valley. They spoke a form of **Abenaki**,
and were related to Indigenous peoples of New Hampshire, Vermont, Maine and eastern
Canada — *not* to the Massachusett/Wampanoag peoples to the south. This matters for
translation: local place names resolve through **Western Abenaki**, not through the
southern New England languages older books used.

**Important correction.** Colonists read Algonquian *place* names as *tribe* names.
Cape Ann History states it plainly: "There actually were no Pawtucket tribes" — the names
identified rivers, villages, and the sagamoreships that occupied them. So "the Pawtucket
tribe" is a colonial artifact; the people are better described as the Pawtucket-Pennacook
people of the lower Merrimack.

**Sagamore vs. sachem** are also not synonyms: a *sagamore* was a chosen member of an
alpha lineage representing shared ancestry; a *sachem* led several related or confederated
sagamoreships.

## 2. Place names

| Name | Modern Abenaki spelling | Meaning | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Quascacunquen** | *Kwaskwaikikwen* | "just right for fields / perfect cropland" | The Parker River and the Newbury village. **Older books say "waterfall" — this is a folk etymology.** Roots: *kwask* (state of being) + *wai* (just right) + *kikwen* (planting fields). |
| **Agawam** | *Akomiwam / Agominam* | "other side of the marsh" | The whole region Merrimack→Salem Harbor. Older gloss "fish-curing place" is contested. |
| **Merrimack** | *Molodemak* | — | Colonial variants: Monumach, Monomack, Monnomacke. |
| **Wenesquawam** | *Wanaskwiwam* | "end of the marsh" | Cape Ann. |
| **Naumkeag** | — | "eel land / here are eels" | Salem–Beverly. |
| **Pennacook** | — | "here are abundant groundnuts" | Per Gordon Day; older gloss "foothill". |

A **1640 Newbury map** labels the Parker River as the **"Quascacunquen River"** and marks
**Indian Hill** at the Artichoke headwaters.

## 3. Where they lived — sites in and near the game map

- **Quascacunquen village** — junction of the Little River with the Parker River in
  Newbury, in the shadow of **Old Town Hill**; crops on the hill's southwest flanks.
  Kent's Island nearby was a ceremonial gathering place. *(Far south — outside the
  practical game frame.)*
- **Smaller settlements all along the Merrimack in Newburyport**, and at the junctions
  with its tributaries.
- **Crane Pond Hill** and **Indian Hill** (West Newbury) — smaller settlements.
- **Shell middens** in the dunes of Plum Island (and Little Neck, Castle Neck, Coffins
  Beach, Wingaersheek).

### Archaeology
- **Newburyport Waterfront, 1977 survey** — stone implements from the Late Archaic,
  **~5,100 BP**. This is the single best anchor: it is *downtown*, where the kids will be.
- **Coffin Stream Collection**, near the Artichoke at Indian Hill (West Newbury) —
  artifacts indicating **~5,000 years of continuous habitation**, Late Archaic through
  Early Woodland.
- **Bull Brook, Ipswich** — occupied **11,500–11,000 years ago**; eight bands harvesting
  migrating caribou. (Out of frame, but the best "how long is a long time" comparison.)
- **Shattuck Farm**, Merrimack bend at Andover — **8,000 BP**.
- **Bannerstones** (Archaic atlatl weights) found in Newbury.

## 4. The year, four homes

The Pawtucket moved with the seasons — this is the most kid-legible fact in the dossier,
and every stop is a place a Newburyport child knows:

- **Spring** — the falls of the Quascacunquen at **Byfield**: fish weirs and eel traps
  during the spawning runs. (Also the great regional gathering at **Pawtucket Falls**,
  present-day Lowell.)
- **Summer** — **Joppa Flats** and **Plum Island**: shellfish, bass, cod.
- **Fall** — back to the planted fields for harvest. Corn, beans and squash by the
  **Three Sisters** method; fields rotated every 2–3 seasons.
- **Winter** — the upland forests, to hunt and trap beaver.

## 5. The Great Dying

- **1616–1619** — an epidemic swept coastal New England. John Smith had counted **over 30
  Pawtucket-Pennacook villages** along the Merrimack in **1614**; afterwards, where "he
  would see hundreds, later there would be scarcely any left, numbering in small bands of
  30 or less." By 1620 the entire Pennacook population was **~2,500**.
- **1633** — a smallpox outbreak followed.
- The English who arrived in **1635** misread fallow fields and empty dwellings as land
  that had always been empty. It had not been. It had been emptied.

## 6. People with names

- **Masconomet** (also Masconomo, Masconomshet) — sagamore of Agawam; his authority ran
  from the **Merrimack to Cape Ann** and inland to North Andover. Submitted to Massachusetts
  Bay rule with three other sagamores by **1634**. Sold Byfield land to Richard Dummer in
  **1634**; deeded Agawam lands to **John Winthrop Jr.** in **1637** and again on
  **28 June 1638**, each for **£20**; confirmed before the General Court **13 March 1639**.
  Died before **18 June 1658**, buried with his gun and tomahawk on **Sagamore Hill** in
  Hamilton. In **1659** a settler named Robert Cross dug up his grave and carried his skull
  through Ipswich on a pole — and was jailed for it. *(Include the grave-robbing only if the
  teacher wants it; it is true, it is documented, and it is a lot for eight-year-olds.)*
- **Old Will** (Pummalancet / Pumpasanoway) — kept a farm and wigwam at Byfield Falls on
  land Henry Sewall claimed. Resisted eviction through the 1660s–70s. In **1679** his family
  **sued under English law** — their lawyer, **Andrew Pittimee**, was himself Indigenous —
  and the settlement let Old Will stay. He **died in his own wigwam in 1684**. His grandson
  **Job Indian** transferred the land in 1681 for £6 13s 4d.
- **Great Tom** — deeded 30 acres near **Indian Hill** to the Newbury selectmen in **1650**
  for £3. Tried to live as the English demanded: fenced his land, converted, paid taxes,
  raised livestock. Fell into debt and indentured himself, his wife and his children.
- **Wonalancet** — son of the great sachem **Passaconaway**, sagamore at Wamesit. In **1675**,
  during King Philip's War, he moved his people north rather than fight. In **1695**, after a
  settler attack, he led the Wamesit survivors to the Abenaki at **St. Francis (Odanak),
  Quebec — where their descendants remain today.**
- **Samuel English**, Masconomet's grandson — signed the **1701** quitclaim for ~10,000 acres
  covering present-day Newbury, for £10.

## 7. Still here

This is the part most local histories get wrong by writing in the past tense.

- Descendants of the Pawtucket live today among **Abenaki, Pequawket, Penobscot and Mi'kmaq**
  communities in Vermont, New Hampshire, Maine and Nova Scotia — including at **Odanak**,
  where Wonalancet's people went in 1695.
- The **Cowasuck Band of Pennacook-Abenaki People** and the **Massachusett Tribe at
  Ponkapoag** are the self-identified groups closest to the historical Pawtucket. (Some
  such claims are disputed; the dossier notes this without adjudicating it.)
- In Essex County itself there is **no surviving community that publicly identifies as
  descended from the pre-1700 people here** — because ancestors were forced to assimilate
  or hid their identity. Absence is evidence of what was done, not of who was here.
- The Pennacook-Pawtucket and Abenaki were **never granted state or federal recognition**;
  only eight New England tribes hold federal recognition.
- Cape Ann History's guidance on acknowledgment: any land acknowledgment in Essex County
  must "in fairness and out of respect for the people who are not here to speak for
  themselves, identify that land as **Pawtucket homeland**."

**The single best kid-facing proof that these people are not gone:** we know what
*Quascacunquen* means because **living Abenaki speakers** can still translate it — and
their translation corrected two centuries of English guessing.

## 8. Open questions / needs review

1. **Quascacunquen's meaning** — "waterfall" (traditional, widely printed) vs. "just right
   for gardens" (Western Abenaki reconstruction, Cape Ann History). The in-game card should
   present the correction as a correction, not silently pick a side. Worth asking a fluent
   speaker.
2. The Cape Ann History translations are **one researcher's reconstruction**, credited in
   part to a language coach (Sasachiminesh). Good, but not a tribal authority.
3. **Placement of the Great Tom story** — his ending (debt, indenture of his children) is
   true and grim. Decide with the teachers how much to carry in a 3rd-grade card.
4. Confirm the 1977 waterfront survey citation against the primary report
   (*Port and market archaeology of the central waterfront, Newburyport*, 1978, govinfo).
