# Newburyport History — Research Dossier

*Researched 2026-06-11 for NBPT Living. For a kids' game (ages 6–12) on an exact map of the city. Sources at bottom.*

## 1. FOUNDING & COLONIAL ERA

- **1633** — English fishermen/traders active at the mouth of the Merrimack before settlement; trader "Great Watt" dug a fish-storage cellar at the foot of today's State Street ("Watt's Cellar," see Legends).
- **1635** — Newbury settled/incorporated by Rev. Thomas Parker and ~100 pioneers from Wiltshire, England, landing along the Parker River (Lower Green, today's Newbury). The riverside "waterside" district at the Merrimack grew into a merchant port.
- **January 28, 1764** — Massachusetts General Court passes "An act for erecting part of the town of Newbury into a new town by the name of Newburyport." Reason: the "waterside people" were "mostly merchants, traders, and artificers" while inland Newbury folk were farmers ("husbandmen"); they quarreled over taxes and church matters.
- **Smallest town in Massachusetts**: at separation, Newburyport = only **647 acres** (~1 sq mi), pop. ~2,800 in 357 houses. Even today (~8.4 sq mi) it's among the smallest cities in the state by area — the "smallest city" claim is a core identity point.
- **1773** — Residents protest the Tea Act by burning British tea in **Market Square** (a local "tea party" claim predating/echoing Boston's).
- **September 1775** — **Benedict Arnold's Quebec expedition**: 1,100 Continental troops marched to Newburyport, camped, and sailed Sept. 19, 1775 on 11 ships arranged by merchant **Nathaniel Tracy** from the waterfront. (Great "fleet departs the docks" set piece.)
- **October 30, 1789** — **George Washington visits**, sleeps at the **Tracy Mansion, 94 State Street** (now the Newburyport Public Library — still standing). Lafayette dined/slept there in 1824; Jefferson, John Adams, Aaron Burr also entertained there.
- **1851** — Newburyport incorporated as a **city**; first mayor Caleb Cushing.

## 2. MARITIME GOLDEN AGE — "CLIPPER CITY"

- By the mid-1700s Newburyport was "the center of New England shipbuilding": **427 vessels launched 1756–1765**. Exports nearly doubled 1791–1794 ($250K→$495K).
- **Shipyards** lined the Merrimack along Merrimac Street/the South End; the famous **Currier & Townsend yard at the foot of Ashland Street** built 51 vessels in 15 years.
- **Donald McKay** — the greatest clipper builder in history — got his start here: partner in **Currier & McKay (1841)** on the Newburyport waterfront; built his first fully self-designed ship (*Courier*, 1842) here before moving to East Boston in 1844. Newburyport claims a disputed "first clipper ship" title and the nickname **"Clipper City."**
- **Clipper Dreadnought (1853)** — built by Currier & Townsend at Ashland Street; 1,414 tons, 210 ft; "the Wild Boat of the Atlantic"; captained by Samuel "Bully" Samuels; 31 NY–Liverpool round trips 1853–64. The city's mascot-grade famous ship.
- **USS Merrimack (1798)** — 24-gun frigate built by Newburyport citizens' subscription and gifted to the Navy; first ship named for the Merrimack River; fought in the Quasi-War under Captain Moses Brown.
- ***Mary L. Cushing* (1883)** — the last square-rigged merchant ship built on the Merrimack.
- **Revolutionary privateers**: Newburyport was a major privateering port (Revolution + War of 1812). Documented ships: **Yankee Hero** (captured a British supply brig; lost June 7, 1776 after a 2-hour fight with frigate HMS *Milford* while outnumbered 4-to-1) and **Vengeance** (Capt. Wingate Newman — captured packet *Harriot*, 14-gun *Snow Eagle*, privateers *Defiance* and *Mary*, and more in one cruise; later burned in the Penobscot Expedition). Financier **Nathaniel Tracy** outfitted dozens of privateers and grew immensely rich (then lost it all).
- **Rum & molasses**: triangle-trade port; molasses in from the West Indies, rum distilled on the waterfront. **Ten distilleries by 1790** (~3,000 hogsheads/year). **Caldwell's "Old Newburyport Rum" (founded 1790)** was the last, distilling on the waterfront until **1961** — barrel-aged with a "butterscotch aroma."
- **Wharves**: the waterfront was a forest of wharves (Ferry Wharf, Bayley's Wharf, Merchant's Row, Central Wharf area — today's Waterfront Park/boardwalk and Custom House area). Whaling era began ~1832.
- **Decline**: Jefferson's **Embargo of 1807**, the **Great Fire of 1811**, and the War of 1812 ended the golden age; the sandbar at the river mouth kept out the biggest new ships.

## 3. BIRTHPLACE OF THE U.S. COAST GUARD

- **USRC Massachusetts**, the **first revenue cutter to enter active service**, was built in Newburyport in **1791** at the **Searle & Tyler shipyard** (William Searle, master builder) — a 2-masted schooner, ~60 ft, ~70 tons, under **Captain John Foster Williams**. The Revenue Cutter Service (founded by Alexander Hamilton, 1790) became the U.S. Coast Guard in 1915.
- Funny true details: she was too expensive for Hamilton, too costly to run for Gen. Benjamin Lincoln, and too slow for her captain — sold October 1792, replaced by the livelier *Massachusetts II*.
- **1965** — Newburyport designated "Birthplace of the U.S. Coast Guard" by presidential proclamation. **2011** — official **Coast Guard City** (14th in the nation), recertified 2022 (ceremony at the Custom House Maritime Museum).
- Today: **USCG Station Merrimack River** (Water Street, by the harbor) holds an **open house with boat tours during Yankee Homecoming** every August; the **Custom House Maritime Museum (25 Water St.)** has a Coast Guard room and hosts CG ceremonies.

## 4. THE GREAT FIRE OF 1811

- Night of **May 31, 1811**, ~9:30 PM, fire started in a **stable on Mechanics' Row (near today's Inn Street)** — blamed on an arsonist, never caught.
- Burned **~16 acres / ~250 wooden buildings** — all of Market Square, State Street, the original **Wolfe Tavern**, shops, printing offices.
- Rebuild law: new construction between Market and Federal Streets had to be **brick with firewalls** — called one of the first unified urban building codes in the U.S. outside Washington D.C.
- Result: today's downtown — **one of the largest collections of Federal-era brick commercial architecture in America** (Market Square Historic District, National Register). The game's downtown map IS the fire's legacy.
- A second "great fire" in 1934 hit downtown but the 1811 one defines the city.

## 5. FAMOUS & COLORFUL FIGURES

- **"Lord" Timothy Dexter (1747–1806)** — America's champion eccentric. Leather-dresser who married rich, bought depreciated Continental currency and got rich when Hamilton redeemed it. Deals that absurdly worked: **warming pans to the West Indies** (sold as molasses ladles), **wool mittens to the Caribbean** (re-exported to Siberia), **coal to Newcastle** (arrived during a miners' strike), **Bibles to the East Indies, stray cats to the Caribbean** (rat control), hoarded **whalebone** (corset boom). Self-published **"A Pickle for the Knowing Ones" (1802)** — 8,847 words, **zero punctuation**; 2nd edition added a page of punctuation marks so readers could "pepper and solt it as they plese." Decked his mansion at **201 High Street (still stands, private home)** with minarets, a gold eagle, and **40+ wooden statues on 15-ft columns** (Washington, Jefferson, Napoleon, Venus, himself — carved by Joseph Wilson; inscription: "I am the first in the East, the first in the West, and the greatest philosopher in the Western World"). **Faked his own funeral** (~3,000 attended), then caned his wife for not crying enough; told guests his living wife was a ghost. Kept fishmonger **Jonathan Plummer as personal poet laureate** ("Lord Dexter is a man of fame; / Most celebrated is his name"). Statues toppled by the 1815 gale; house gutted by a 1988 blowtorch accident, rebuilt exactly. Buried in **Old Hill Burying Ground**.
- **William Lloyd Garrison (1805–1879)** — fiery abolitionist, born on **School Street** (house still stands near Old South Church). Dirt-poor child who sold candy and begged food; **apprentice printer at the Newburyport Herald at 13**, its editor by 1824 (paper founded 1797). Founded **The Liberator** (1831) in Boston, demanded immediate emancipation for 35 years. **Bronze statue (1893, sculptor David M. French) in Brown Square** — still there, facing the Garrison Inn.
- **George Whitefield (1714–1770)** — superstar Great Awakening preacher (crowds of 20,000+). Died **Sept. 30, 1770** in the Federal Street parsonage; buried **in a crypt directly under the pulpit of Old South Church (29 Federal St.)** — visitors can still descend to see it. In **1829 a visitor stole his arm bone**; it was mailed back from England ~20 years later in a little wooden box and re-interred before 2,000 people. His mummified **thumb** ended up at the Methodist Archives in Madison, NJ. The church's bell was **cast by Paul Revere (1816)**.
- **Caleb Cushing (1800–1879)** — Newburyport's overachiever: Congressman, **first mayor of Newburyport (1851)**, negotiated the **Treaty of Wanghia with China (July 3, 1844)** — America's first China treaty — U.S. Attorney General, diplomat. NOT an abolitionist (useful contrast with Garrison). Family home = **Cushing House, 98 High St.**, now the Museum of Old Newbury.
- **Andrew J. "Bossy" Gillis (1896–1965)** — red-headed gas-station owner and brawling populist mayor. **Ran for mayor ~20 times, won 6** between 1928 and 1959. Blocked from building his gas station at High & State, he decorated the lot with tombstones and junk labeled "The Spirit of Newburyport," **punched Mayor Cashman in the jaw, and served 2 months in jail** — then got elected mayor. Later jailed again over gas-station violations while in office; ran City Hall from his gas station; painted enemies' names on toilet plumbing displayed on his building; yelled insults across Market Square. TIME magazine: he fit old Newburyport "like a prime bull at a vegetarians' convention."
- **Jacob Perkins (1766–1849)** — Newburyport-born inventor: coin dies at 21, **nail-making machine (1790/95)**, counterfeit-proof **steel-plate banknote engraving**, and the first vapor-compression **refrigeration** description — "father of the refrigerator." 40 patents.
- **Others**: Francis Cabot Lowell (textile pioneer, b. Newburyport 1775); novelist John P. Marquand (Pulitzer 1938, lived at Curzon Mill); Captain Samuel "Bully" Samuels (Dreadnought); Capt. Moses Brown (USS Merrimack); merchant Moses Brown (Brown Square's namesake); "Madam" Sarah Emery (memoirist); Ghlee Woodworth's "Tiptoe Through the Tombstones" tours today.

## 6. INDUSTRY

- **Silver**: **William Moulton II began silversmithing ~1690** — start of the longest silversmith dynasty in America (Moultons on State Street 1764–1810). Apprentices became **Towle & Jones (1857)** → **Towle Manufacturing Co. (1882)** — Towle Silversmiths, famous nationwide, operated in Newburyport into the late 20th century (former factory on Merrimac Street).
- **Combs**: **Enoch Noyes of West Newbury (1759)** made combs/buttons from heated cattle horn; grew into ~30 comb shops; **W.H. Noyes & Bros. factory** (Chestnut Street block, 1880–1933) made **400 dozen horn combs a day** with 85 workers in the 1890s.
- **Shoes**: by the mid-1850s, **9 of 10 Newburyport families had someone in the shoe factories**; shoe, comb, and silver manufacturing replaced shipping.
- **The Tannery**: leather tanning (harnesses, saddles, shoe leather) on the Prince Place/Water Street site; Prime Tanning sold out 1985; redeveloped into today's **Tannery Marketplace (50 Water St.)** — shops in old factory buildings.
- **Eastern Railroad (1840)** — reached Newburyport with a downtown wooden station near Washington Street; today's MBTA Newburyport line is its descendant. A separate **Newburyport Railroad** ran inland to Wakefield.
- **Newburyport Turnpike (Route 1)** — opened **Feb. 11, 1805**, built dead-straight Boston→Newburyport for $500,000; mocked for going "over every hill and missing every town"; a financial flop but now U.S. Route 1.
- Also: textile steam mills, Merrimack Arms "Southerner" Derringer pistols (1867–73), horse streetcars 1870 → electric trolleys 1891, **Chipman/Merrimac silver-lead MINE on Scotland Road (Newbury), discovered Aug. 1874** by farmer Albert Adams — yielded ~$500,000 in silver by 1879, brief "silver rush" with a parade; reopened 1919–25.

## 7. ABOLITION & UNDERGROUND RAILROAD

- Garrison is the headline; his statue (Brown Square) and birthplace (School Street) are the anchors.
- **Documented UGRR conductors**: **Richard Plumer** (dry-goods merchant; hid fugitives in the barn behind his house; son Wendell rode night wagons), **Captain Alexander Graves** (smuggled freedom-seekers north by sea from southern ports; Marine Society member 1847), **William Jackman** (house near the Chain Bridge; drove fugitives to Amesbury or Lee, NH — often toward poet John Greenleaf Whittier in Amesbury).
- Route: arrivals from Ipswich hidden under grain sacks in wagons → through town → **Turkey Hill Farm** above the Artichoke River (last waystation) → north. One documented escape: a group fled bounty hunters into a cornfield en route to Turkey Hill and all got away.
- **Myth-bust**: the downtown tunnels were almost certainly for smuggling, NOT the Underground Railroad — no fugitive testimony mentions tunnels.
- The Black history of **Old Hill Burying Ground**: 18 unmarked graves of early Black residents found by ground-penetrating radar (2017–2023), now honored with granite "Once Known" markers — a respectful, real discovery story.

## 8. PLUM ISLAND

- **Lights**: **Newburyport Harbor Light first lit April 14, 1788** — 13th lighthouse in the U.S. and likely the country's **first range lights**; original towers sat on wooden skids and were **dragged ~3,000 feet over the years to chase the shifting sandbar**. Current **45-ft wooden tower built 1898** (lens first lit Sept. 20, 1898), on **Northern Boulevard at the island's north tip — still standing**, grounds open, periodic tours by Friends of Plum Island Light. Washington appointed first keeper **Abner Lowell** — three generations of Lowells kept the light. Automated 1951.
- **Shipwrecks**: 100+ serious wrecks on these shores in the 1700s–1800s. **December 1839**: three storms wrecked 300+ vessels region-wide, 150+ dead. The brig **Pocahontas wrecked Dec. 23, 1839** near the Plum Island Hotel — all hands lost; crew buried under a marble monument **at the gate of Old Hill Burying Ground** (still there). Longfellow's "The Wreck of the Hesperus" was inspired by these 1839 storms.
- **Life-saving**: shoreline survival shack with blankets/water predated organized rescue; first **Life-Saving Station 1874** (later U.S. Life-Saving Service/Coast Guard stations; predecessor of Station Merrimack River, plus the 1890 Knobbs Beach station).
- **Hotel/resort era**: toll road (Plum Island Turnpike) + first **Plum Island Hotel, 1806** (corner of today's Plum Island Turnpike & Northern Blvd.); enlarged to 48 rooms with tower in 1885; steamboats up the river, **horse railroad 1887 → electric trolley 1894 → tracks ripped up 1922**. Hotel torn down 1914.
- **Plum Island Airport (2B2)** — at the Newbury end of Plum Island Turnpike: **New England's oldest flying field**. **April–Aug 1910, W. Starling Burgess & Augustus Herring** test-flew their biplane **"Flying Fish"** off the marsh (first flights April 17, 1910). Field formalized 1926 (Army emergency field), commercial 1933; **Burgess Aviation Museum** on site (since 2002). Still an active grass airfield.
- **Pink House (60 Plum Island Turnpike)** — lone pink 1925 house in the marsh; legend says a divorce "spite house" plumbed with salt water; really built by Gertrude Cutter. Beloved landmark, **demolished by US Fish & Wildlife March 11, 2025** — survives only as a memory/memorial (April 2026: granite-post memorial sign with painting). Use respectfully as a "vanished landmark" quest.
- **Today**: Parker River National Wildlife Refuge (est. 1942) covers most of the island; Mass Audubon **Joppa Flats Education Center** on the Turnpike; famous purple sand streaks (garnet) on the beach.

## 9. LEGENDS, MYSTERIES & KID-FRIENDLY SPOOKY LORE (documented)

- **Tunnels under downtown — REAL**: at least three known brick tunnels (**under Market Street, near State Street, and Federal Street**); one was unearthed during construction near the riverfront ale house. Best theory: smuggling rum and goods after the hated **Embargo of 1807** and during the War of 1812. Entrances are sealed; nobody knows the full map. (Perfect dungeon network.)
- **The Morse Poltergeist, 1679–80** — a dozen years BEFORE Salem: at the **William & Elizabeth Morse house on the southeast side of Market Square**, stones rained on the roof, pots and trays flew, objects vanished. Sailor "wizard" **Caleb Powell** said the trickster was grandson **John Stiles** (very likely true!), but Powell got accused, then grandmother **Elizabeth Morse was convicted of witchcraft and sentenced to hang — reprieved** after a year in Boston jail, confined near her home. Small **plaque at 22 Market St./Liberty St. side** (placed 1999). A ready-made mystery quest with a kid culprit.
- **Charles Street School poltergeist, September 1871** — knocking, pounding, door-raps with no one there, stove covers levitating in the boys' primary school; covered in the press.
- **Garrison Inn ghosts (11 Brown Square)** — builder Moses Brown said to appear by the tavern archways; "Sarah" in room 408; staff report flying objects and whispers.
- **"Harry" the mooncusser of Plum Island** — ghost of a land-pirate said to wander the dunes before storms (mooncussers lured ships aground with false lights).
- **Sea serpent, 1817–1819** — the famous Gloucester sea serpent was reported up and down the coast; the **Newburyport Herald printed serpent reports Aug. 26, 1817**. Fair game for an offshore sighting mini-game.
- **Watt's Cellar — the lost first structure**: "Great Watt's" 1630s fish cellar at the foot of State Street is the oldest documented structure in the whole Merrimack Valley — **archaeologists dug for it in 1977 near today's Firehouse and never conclusively found it**. A real buried treasure hunt.
- **Whitefield's stolen arm (1829)** and returned-in-a-box (c. 1849) — true crypt tale, parade of 2,000 at reburial.
- **Dexter's fake funeral** — see above; his ghost-wife gag too.
- **H.P. Lovecraft** modeled creepy "Innsmouth" partly on a decaying 1920s Newburyport (older-kid Easter egg).
- **Treasure**: pirate-treasure rumors cluster on Plum Island dunes (never verified); the real "treasure" stories are the 1874 silver mine and smugglers' tunnels.

## TOP GAME HOOKS (anchored to real places)

1. **The Smugglers' Tunnels** — map and explore the real sealed brick tunnels under Market/State/Federal Streets, dodging 1812-era smugglers' puzzles (anchor: Market Square cellars).
2. **Lord Dexter's Impossible Shopping List** — fetch-quest chain: sell warming pans, mittens, Bibles, cats, and coal to the right ships before they sail (anchor: Dexter House, 201 High St. + waterfront wharves).
3. **The Missing Statues** — the 1815 gale scattered Dexter's 40 wooden statues; find them all over the map as collectibles and restore his garden (anchor: 201 High St.).
4. **Punctuation Rescue** — mini-game inserting commas and periods into pages of "A Pickle for the Knowing Ones" so townsfolk can read them (anchor: printing office on State St.).
5. **The Fake Funeral** — help stage (or see through) Dexter's mock wake without laughing; NPC crowd of 3,000 (anchor: Dexter House lawn).
6. **Race the Dreadnought** — sailing time-trial as the "Wild Boat of the Atlantic" with Captain "Bully" Samuels (anchor: Currier & Townsend shipyard, foot of Ashland St.).
7. **Launch the Massachusetts** — shipbuilding mini-game assembling the first revenue cutter rib-by-rib, then a maiden patrol catching smugglers (anchor: Searle & Tyler yard site / Custom House Museum).
8. **The Great Fire Bucket Brigade** — night of May 31, 1811: pass buckets, rescue the Wolfe Tavern sign, then rebuild downtown in brick (tile-placement mini-game) (anchor: Inn Street stable → Market Square).
9. **Underground Railroad Night Run** — stealth wagon ride hiding under grain sacks from Plumer's barn to Turkey Hill Farm, evading bounty hunters (anchors: Federal St. area, Turkey Hill).
10. **Young Garrison's Paper Route** — deliver the Newburyport Herald as 13-year-old apprentice Lloyd (anchors: School St. birthplace, Brown Square statue).
11. **The Mystery of Watt's Cellar** — archaeology dig for the lost 1630s fish cellar somewhere near the Firehouse (anchor: Market Square).
12. **The Market Square Poltergeist (1679)** — investigate flying pots, clear innocent Grandma Morse, catch the real prankster — her grandson (anchor: plaque at 22 Market St.).
13. **The Preacher's Stolen Arm** — track the bone thief of 1829 and escort the little wooden box home to the crypt beneath the pulpit (anchor: Old South Church, 29 Federal St.).
14. **Keeper of the Walking Lighthouse** — drag the lighthouse on log rollers to chase the shifting sandbar, then keep the lamp lit through the 1839 triple storm to save the brig Pocahontas (anchor: Plum Island Light).
15. **Harry the Mooncusser** — spot the false lights on the dunes before storms and out-trick the ghost of the land-pirate (anchor: Plum Island dunes).
16. **First Flight of the Flying Fish** — 1910 physics mini-game getting Burgess's biplane off the marsh in hops (anchor: Plum Island Airport).
17. **Bossy Gillis's Goat-Rodeo Campaign** — comic election mini-game: gas-station mayor vs. the fancy "fossils" (anchor: High & State Sts. / City Hall).
18. **Silver Rush of 1874** — pickaxe mini-game in the Chipman mine; strike the vein, join the silver parade downtown (anchor: Scotland Road; Towle silver tie-in on Merrimac St.).
19. **Benedict Arnold's Secret Fleet** — provision 11 ships in secret and slip 1,100 soldiers past British patrols out the river mouth (anchors: waterfront wharves + Tracy Mansion/Library).
20. **Frog Pond Frenzy + George Washington's Visit** — catch frogs at Frog Pond while the town readies for Washington's 1789 parade (anchors: Bartlet Mall, Tracy Mansion).

Bonus collectible systems: horn combs (comb factory), silver spoons (Towle/Moulton), root-beer barrels (Caldwell's wharf, kid-ified), clipper-ship cards (shipyards), clams at Joppa Flats, purple garnet sand vials (Plum Island beach), bald-eagle photo spots (Maudslay/Deer Island).
